The maths, step by step

What the site and the French article compute, explained for readers who are not statisticians: what a distribution is, why the plots use logarithmic axes and what they would look like without them, what the model p(n) ∝ n−βe−λn and its two parameters mean, how the fit is done and how sure we can be of it, and the statistics a statistical reviewer would ask for. All the numbers below are computed on the data of the article: the five French corpora, SUD trees, units of 10–19 words (written corpora re-segmented); “micro” means microsyntax only, i.e. macro-syntactic segments cut out as in the article.

1. A distribution is a histogram

Take a measure, say the flux size, and a set of sentences. At every position between two words, the flux has a value n = 1, 2, 3, … Count how many positions have each value. These counts, divided by the total number of positions N, are the probabilities p(n): they sum to 1. The mean is Σ n·p(n); the mode is the most frequent value; the tail is the set of large, rare values. For dependency length, the observations are dependencies instead of positions, but the arithmetic is the same (and, with all relations kept, mean length = mean flux, see Measures). Choose a running example; the next three sections use it.

Points: observed probabilities (hollow when fewer than 5 observations). Line: the fitted model of section 3.

Linear axes show the bulk of the distribution and hide the tail: a value with probability 0.5 % is half a pixel high, and everything beyond the mode looks like “nothing”. Yet the tail is what the whole study is about: how fast do large flux values become rare?

A logarithmic y axis gives the same vertical space to each factor of 10: 10 %, 1 %, 0.1 %, 0.01 % are evenly spaced. A tail that decays exponentially, p(n) ∝ e−λn, becomes a straight line: each extra unit of n divides the probability by the same factor eλ, so each step down is the same height. The slope of that line is λ (in natural-log units; on a base-10 axis the line drops λ/2.30 decades per unit).

Log–log axes also give equal space to each factor of 10 on the x axis: 1, 10, 100 are evenly spaced. A power law, p(n) ∝ n−β, becomes a straight line of slope −β: doubling n always divides the probability by the same factor 2β, wherever you are. This is what dependency length looks like over most of its range, which is why the length plots of this site and of the literature (Ferrer-i-Cancho 2004; Petrini & Ferrer-i-Cancho 2025) are drawn this way: a long tail that is still roughly straight at length 30 or 40. A power law with an exponential cut-off is a straight line that bends downwards at the right; a distribution that rises to a mode and then falls (flux, weight) is a hump.

Which one to use? For dependency length (values from 1 to 30 and beyond) log–log is the informative choice. For flux size and weight the values run only from 1 to about 9: on a log x axis they are squeezed into less than one decade, the points bunch up on the right and the hump is distorted; a linear x with a log y shows the same information more honestly. The French article draws Figure 2 in log–log for consistency with the length plots; the statistician's review asks for linear x, and this page shows both so that the choice can be made knowingly. Whatever the axes, the numbers (means, λ) do not change: only what the eye can see.

3. The model, term by term

p(n) = C · n−β · e−λn   for n = 1, 2, …, nmax
  • e−λn, the exponential part. Going from n to n + 1 multiplies the probability by e−λ, the same factor at every step: λ = 0.5 divides by 1.6, λ = 1 by 2.7, λ = 2 by 7.4, λ = 4 by 55. The larger λ, the faster large values disappear. λ is the cut-off, or decay rate.
  • n−β, the power-law part. Going from n to n + 1 multiplies the probability by ((n+1)/n)−β, a factor that is strong for small n (from 1 to 2: 2−β) and fades for large n (from 10 to 11: 1.1−β). With β > 0 it pulls the distribution down at the start and lets it decay slowly afterwards: a long tail. With β < 0 it pushes the distribution up at the start: the curve rises until n ≈ −β/λ (the mode) and then the exponential takes over. This is the shape of flux and weight.
  • C, the normalising constant: the probabilities must sum to 1 over the values that can occur, 1 to nmax (the largest value observed in that cell). C is just 1 divided by the sum of the unnormalised weights n−βe−λn; it has no interpretation, but its dependence on nmax is one of the statistician's points (section 4).

Why this family? Because one formula covers the three shapes we meet: dependency length is close to a power law with a soft cut-off (β ≈ 1.8, λ ≈ 0.1 in GSD); flux size rises to a mode of 2 and then decays fast (β ≈ −3, λ ≈ 2); weight is almost all 1 and 2 and decays even faster (β ≈ −6, λ ≈ 4.6). The special cases are: λ = 0, a pure power law; β = 0, a pure exponential (a geometric distribution on integers); β < 0, a discrete gamma shape. The last name matters: when β is negative the model is not a power law at all, and the review is right that “truncated power law” is then a misnomer. 4. The fit: maximum likelihood

How do we choose β and λ? By maximum likelihood: among all pairs (β, λ), take the one under which the observed counts were the most probable. If the model gives probability p(n) to value n, and value n was observed cn times, the probability of the whole sample is the product of p(n)cn over all n. Products of thousands of small numbers are unmanageable, so one takes the logarithm and changes the sign:

NLL(β, λ) = − Σn cn · log p(n | β, λ)

The negative log-likelihood NLL is a misfit score: the smaller, the better. The computer searches for the minimum (here with the Nelder–Mead method, started from four different points to avoid getting stuck). The readout under the sliders above shows NLL(β, λ) − NLLmin: how much worse your hand-made curve is than the best one. A difference of 2 is already a clearly worse model; a difference of 1.92 is the conventional edge of the 95 % interval (section 6).

The landscape, and why β and λ are not independent

Misfit NLL(β, λ) − NLLmin for the flux size of GSD (all relations). The star is the best fit; contour levels double from 1 to 1024.

The valley is diagonal: a larger λ with a more negative β gives almost the same curve as a smaller λ with a less negative β. The two parameters trade off; this is what the review means by “β and λ are strongly correlated”. Consequences: λ on its own does not describe the shape, so the article should print β next to it; the standard error of λ is larger than it would be if β were known; and two corpora can have different λ but nearly the same distribution. The Cut-off λ page reports the same thing between languages as a correlation of −0.8 between λ and the mean.

The dependence on nmax

The probabilities are normalised on 1 … nmax. If one chooses a larger support than the observed maximum, the model has to put some mass on values that never occur, and the fit changes. The table refits the same counts with different nmax:

Why not a regression on the log counts?

The June pages fitted the straight line of log p(n) against (log n, n) by ordinary least squares. That gives every observed value the same weight: the point n = 9, observed three times, counts as much as n = 2, observed forty thousand times, so the estimate is pulled by the noisiest points. Maximum likelihood weighs each observation, which is what we want. On these corpora the two methods agree for dependency length, whose tail has many points, and differ by 0.3 to 0.6 for flux and weight, where OLS fits a line through only six or seven points (table). The site uses maximum likelihood; the regression estimates remain available as an option in the tables.

5. Reading λ: an asymptotic rate, not the ratio you see

With β ≠ 0, the factor from n to n + 1 is ((n+1)/n)−β·e−λ: it only converges to e−λ as n grows. For flux (β ≈ −3) the fitted ratio at n = 2 is much larger than e−λ, and the observed distribution is still rising there. So λ is the decay rate of the far tail, and “each extra unit divides the probability by eλ” is only true far out, where there are few data. For flux weight, the observed ratios fall faster and faster (about 0.34, then 0.04, then 0.007): the decay is faster than exponential. The fitted model bends too, but only through a very negative β (about −7), and its asymptotic factor e−λ (about 0.002) lies far below every ratio actually observed: λweight then describes no step seen in the data and is best read as an index of how sharply the weight is bounded, not as a rate. The psycholinguist's point about “a limit rather than a rate” is this observation; section 7 shows a family whose ratios can keep falling.

λ and the mean, and the random baseline

A corpus with a larger mean flux usually has a smaller λ: the two measure related things (how far the distribution extends). So a difference in λ between two corpora may just restate a difference in means. The answer used on the full flux site is a random baseline: put the words of each sentence in a random projective order, keep the same tree, and fit again. 6. How sure are we? Standard errors

A standard error (SE) answers: if we had another corpus of the same kind and size, by how much would the estimate typically move? About 95 % of the time, the true value lies within ±2 SE of the estimate (the 95 % confidence interval). Two estimates are said to differ significantly (at the 5 % level) when their difference exceeds about 2 × √(SEa² + SEb²). Three ways to get an SE for λ:

  1. Curvature of the likelihood (the Hessian, the second derivatives of NLL at the minimum): a sharp valley means a precise estimate. This assumes that every position is an independent draw. It is not: the positions of one sentence rise and fall together (a long sentence with a deep embedding has many high positions). The Hessian SE is therefore too small.
  2. Profile likelihood: for each candidate λ, refit β and record the misfit; the 95 % interval is where the misfit exceeds the minimum by less than 1.92 (half of the 3.84 of a χ² with one degree of freedom). It accounts for the β–λ trade-off, but still assumes independence.
  3. Sentence bootstrap: draw sentences at random with replacement until the corpus has its original size, refit, repeat 200 times; the spread of the 200 λ is the SE. It respects the dependence between positions of a sentence; on the whole-language distributions of this site it gives errors 3 to 8 times the Hessian ones, in the 10–19-word cells of the French corpora (table). This is what the French article reports.

7. Comparing two distributions

Total variation distance

TV(p, q) = ½ Σ |p(n) − q(n)| is the largest share of probability that one would have to move to turn one distribution into the other; 0 means identical, 1 means disjoint. The article reports a largest TV of 0.012 between the ContentUD weight distributions of the four corpora and calls them “practically identical”. The review's objection is that 0.012 has no scale until we know what TV two samples from the same distribution would show by chance. Simulation gives that noise floor:

The distributions are very close; whether they are identical is not what a distance can tell. A χ² test on the counts (corpora × values 1, 2, ≥ 3) asks that question: it rejects identity (), but the effect size Cramér's V is tiny. With tens of thousands of positions, almost any difference is “significant”; the question is whether it is large, and for that one needs an effect size or an equivalence test: decide beforehand a margin δ below which two distributions count as the same, and show that the confidence interval of the difference lies inside ±δ.

Likelihood-ratio test: one model or two?

To test whether two corpora have the same distribution, fit the model to both together (one β, one λ: NLLpooled) and to each separately (NLLa + NLLb). Separate fits always do at least as well; if the corpora really share the distribution, the gain LR = 2 (NLLpooled − NLLa − NLLb) behaves like a χ² with 2 degrees of freedom (two extra parameters), so LR > 6 is significant at 5 %.

With these sample sizes, every pair differs significantly for the flux size, including the two written corpora, and most pairs for the weight: a test answers “is there any difference?”, not “does it matter?”. The article's claims should be phrased as sizes of differences with intervals, not as presence or absence.

Which family fits best? The AIC

The Akaike information criterion, AIC = 2k + 2 NLL, trades misfit against the number of parameters k: a model with one more parameter must reduce NLL by more than 1 to be preferred. Lower is better; a difference of 10 or more is decisive. The review suggests families made for counts with a positive mode. Fitted on the same counts (all normalised on 1 … nmax):

Families: geometric, p(n) ∝ e−λn (constant ratio); power law, ∝ n−β; truncated power law (the model of the site); shifted Poisson, n − 1 ~ Poisson(μ), one parameter, ratios that fall like μ/n (faster than exponential); shifted negative binomial, a Poisson whose rate varies, two parameters, between Poisson and geometric; discrete Weibull, two parameters, whose ratio can fall or rise with n. What matters here is not the winner but the lesson: for dependency length the truncated power law is clearly best; for flux size and weight the discrete Weibull, whose ratios keep falling, equals or beats it (decisively for the flux of GSD), which is the quantitative form of “is it a rate or a limit?”.

8. Controlling for sentence length

All measures grow with sentence length, so the article compares sentences of 10–19 words. The review's point: inside that bin the corpora are not equally distributed. Written corpora have long sentences, so their 10–19-word sentences crowd towards 19; spoken corpora have short units, so theirs crowd towards 10. If the mean flux rises by about 0.035 per word, a corpus whose units are one word shorter on average gets a mean flux 0.035 lower for that reason alone, which is the size of the differences in Table 2 without macro-syntax. The cure is to compare at each exact length (below), or to fit a regression fluxs = a + b·lengths + c·corpuss per sentence, where c is the corpus effect at equal length; a mixed model adds a random term per speaker or text, so that a few talkative speakers do not count as thousands of independent sentences.

9. Glossary

Distribution, histogram
For each possible value, how often it occurs; as probabilities, the counts divided by their total.
Support
The set of values a distribution can take; here 1 … nmax.
Normalisation
Scaling the model so that its probabilities sum to 1 over the support.
Mean, mode, median, tail
The average value; the most frequent value; the value that splits the data in half; the region of large, rare values.
Logarithm, log scale
log10(1000) = 3: the logarithm counts factors of 10. A log axis gives equal space to equal factors; e is the base of natural logarithms (2.718…), used in e−λn.
Exponential decay
Each unit step divides the probability by the same factor; a straight line on a log y axis.
Power law
Each doubling divides the probability by the same factor; a straight line on log–log axes; “scale-free”.
Parameter
A number that selects one curve in a family: β and λ here.
Likelihood, NLL, MLE
The probability of the observed data under given parameters; its negative logarithm, used as a misfit score; the parameters that minimise it (maximum-likelihood estimate).
Hessian
The matrix of second derivatives of NLL at the minimum; its inverse gives standard errors that assume independent observations.
Bootstrap
Re-creating many fake samples by drawing from the real one with replacement, to see how much an estimate moves.
Standard error, confidence interval
The typical sampling spread of an estimate; the range (about ±2 SE) that contains the true value 95 % of the time.
p-value, significance
The probability of a difference at least as large as the observed one if there were no real difference; below 0.05, “significant”. Says nothing about the size of the difference.
χ² (chi-square), degrees of freedom
A statistic comparing observed and expected counts, whose reference distribution depends on the number of free cells (degrees of freedom); also the reference distribution of the likelihood-ratio statistic.
Effect size, Cramér's V
How large a difference is, independently of the sample size; V rescales χ² between 0 and 1.
AIC
2k + 2 NLL: misfit plus a penalty for the number of parameters; lower is better.
Total variation distance
Half the sum of absolute differences between two probability distributions; the share of mass to move.
Likelihood-ratio test
Compares a model to a more general one containing it, through the gain in log-likelihood.
Equivalence test
Shows that a difference is smaller than a margin fixed in advance, instead of failing to show that it is non-zero.
Regression, covariate, mixed model
A model of one quantity as a function of others (the covariates, e.g. sentence length); a mixed model adds random effects for groups such as speakers.
Simpson's paradox, confounding
A comparison that reverses or vanishes once a third variable (here, length within the bin) is controlled.
Random baseline
What the measure would be if the words of each sentence were put in a random projective order with the same tree.
